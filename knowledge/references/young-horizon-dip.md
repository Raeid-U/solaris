## Scope

This source explains how observer height changes the apparent horizon and how refraction modifies that effect.

## Extracted Takeaways

- The observer's eye height is small compared with Earth's radius, but the resulting dip of the horizon is not negligible for naked-eye horizon observations.
- Without refraction, geometric dip can be approximated by `sqrt(2h/R)` where `h` is observer height and `R` is Earth's radius.
- Refraction lets an observer see farther and changes the apparent dip.
- A common practical dip approximation cited on the page is proportional to `sqrt(h)` in meters.
- The source stresses that refraction depends on temperature gradient and real atmospheric conditions.

## Formulas / Locators

- "Dip, without refraction": geometric derivation and `sqrt(2h/R)` approximation.
- "Dip, including refraction": effective-radius framing using ray curvature.
- "Dip and Temperature": practical dependence on temperature gradient and atmospheric state.

## Assumptions

- Earth is treated geometrically with radius `R`.
- The simple no-refraction formula ignores atmospheric ray bending.
- The refraction discussion uses simplifying assumptions for explanatory purposes.

## Limitations

- Local terrain, buildings, mountains, coastlines, and obstructions are separate from geometric sea-horizon dip.
- Refraction can vary substantially with weather, time of day, water/land temperature contrast, and inversions.
